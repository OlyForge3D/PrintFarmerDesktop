# A wrong reason that predicts the right table

A measurement can be correct, reproducible, and correctly reported, and the
explanation offered alongside it can still be false. This is the most durable
error a careful process produces, because the standard remedy for doubt — run it
again — confirms the table every time and never touches the story attached to it.

The two are not equally dangerous. A table is local: it says what happened in the
cases that were run. An explanation is general: it says why, and therefore makes
claims about cases nobody ran. Explanations are also what travel. When a result
is relayed, summarised, or built upon, the reason is the part that survives the
retelling, and it is the part that licenses the next decision. So the component
with the widest reach is the one carrying no evidence, and it arrives wearing the
credibility earned by the measurement it is standing next to.

The failure is often presentational before it is substantive. Measured facts and
the reasoning about them get written in one voice, in one paragraph, without
marking which is which. A reader cannot separate them afterwards, and neither can
the author. The discipline is to state, at the point of writing, which sentences
report what was observed and which propose why — not because the reasoning is
untrustworthy, but because only the marked version can later be tested
separately.

The detection method is to convert the explanation into a prediction that could
fail, and then build the case that would break it. An explanation that generates
no prediction cannot be caught being wrong; it can only be caught being useless.
This means the useful response to a plausible reason is not agreement but
extraction: what would have to be true elsewhere if this reason held, and what
observation would contradict it. The arm built to break the reason needs its own
control, for the ordinary cause — an arm with no divergent control is not a
refutation, it is an unrun experiment.

The same error costs more when the false reason is attached to a capability
rather than to a result. A reason explains why a measurement came out as it did,
and a wrong one distorts the next inference. A reason offered for why something
cannot be done closes the search entirely, and nothing downstream ever reopens
it, because the conclusion it supports is usually true. A negative result
obtained from two attempts, generalised to a mechanism, will be repeated as a
property of the system by people who never ran even those two. The tell is a
universal quantifier that no measurement could have established: never, cannot,
not available to us. Where the evidence is a handful of specific failures, the
claim must name those failures and stop, because the untested remainder is
exactly where the capability turns out to live.

An asymmetry follows that is worth stating plainly. A prediction derived from an
explanation and then falsified is a better outcome than a prediction that holds,
because it is the only one that produces the discriminating case. Agreement
between a reason and the cases already run is not evidence for the reason; those
cases are what the reason was fitted to. Only a case selected specifically
because the reason forbids it can distinguish a correct explanation from one that
happens to sit on top of correct data.

Recorded from the reviewer's own error rather than from anyone's code. A
four-armed experiment produced a table that was exact and has since been
independently reproduced. The explanation attached to one row was wrong, and it
was wrong in the direction that made the finding sound more important — it
described a safety property as accidental and unprotected when the property is in
fact structural. Nobody could have caught it by re-running the four arms, all of
which agree with the false reason. It was caught only because a second reader
turned the reason into a prediction about a case that had not been run, and a
fifth arm built to test that prediction falsified the reason while leaving every
row of the table standing. The direction of the error is the part worth
remembering: a wrong reason that inflates the importance of one's own result is
the one least likely to be examined by the person who benefits from it, and most
likely to be quoted by everyone else.
